H1: A Cary chiropractor on specific chiropractic: accuracy over force
Ever tried hammering a nail into a board? As a kid I helped my dad with projects around the house, and he would toss me scrap wood to practice on. Good carpenters make it look effortless. At 8 years old, I can tell you it was not. I missed plenty and dented the wood, and when I got frustrated I swung harder. Any good carpenter will tell you it is not about strength; it is about accuracy. A swinging hammer already has the force to drive the nail as long as it lands on target. A harder swing that misses might as well be a mile off. The only way to sink a nail is to hit it squarely.

## Chiropractic works the same way
People have manipulated the spine and moved bones for ages. In 1856, D.D. Palmer became the first to adjust the spine with the hammer-and-nail idea in mind. His adjustment was about precision, not force. He moved a single bone and created a total change in his patient's nervous system: a man who had been deaf for 17 years could hear again. Before that, spinal manipulation was more "pop and pray," a lot like my early days with a hammer. Had Palmer been off by a few millimeters, his patient most likely would not have been able to hear when he got up from the table.

Chiropractic rests on a philosophy, a science and an art. Understanding and applying all three is what lets a chiropractor hit the mark.
